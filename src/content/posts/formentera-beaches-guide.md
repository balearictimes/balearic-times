Formentera is the smallest inhabited Balearic island, around 19 kilometres from end to end, and its main attraction is its coast. The water is shallow and exceptionally clear over pale sand, which is why the island is often compared with far-off tropical places. Seagrass meadows protect the water quality, and the beaches stay clean as a result.

## Ses Illetes and Llevant

Ses Illetes is on the long, narrow peninsula of Trucadors, at the northern tip of the island. It is the best-known beach on Formentera and often appears in lists of the best in Europe. The sea is shallow and turquoise, and the sand is fine and white. On the other side of the peninsula lies Platja de Llevant, which is a little wilder and usually a bit less crowded.

Both beaches are inside a protected natural park. There are restaurants and beach clubs, and the car park fills early in high season. Many people come by bike or on a boat trip from Ibiza.

## Espalmador

At the end of the peninsula, a narrow channel separates Formentera from the small island of S'Espalmador. It has its own beaches and a pool of mud that visitors like to smear on their skin. The crossing is shallow in places, and people sometimes wade across, but currents and conditions vary, so take care and think about the return trip. Many visitors go by boat instead.

## Platja de Migjorn

Platja de Migjorn is a long, quiet stretch of sand running along the south coast for around five kilometres. It has several beach bars and restaurants at intervals, and it is easy to walk far enough to find space. The sea can be rougher here when the wind is up, so check conditions.

## Cala Saona

Cala Saona, on the west coast, is a sheltered bay with fine sand and a calm sea. It faces west, which makes it popular for sunset. The area has a few hotels, a restaurant and a beach bar.

## Cavall d'en Borràs and the south-west

Cavall d'en Borràs is a rocky stretch of coast on the south-west of the island, with fishermen's huts along the shore. The sea here is clear and suited to snorkelling, but there is little sand.

## Es Caló

Es Caló is a small fishing village on the north-east coast of the island, with a narrow beach and some old boat sheds. It has a relaxed feel and is a good place for a lunch of fresh fish.

## Practical tips

- Formentera has no airport, and the ferry from Ibiza docks at La Savina. Bikes and scooters are available to hire near the port.
- In July and August, go to the most popular beaches early or late, since midday is crowded.
- Protect the seagrass. Boats should not anchor on it, and visitors should not collect it or dig in the sand.
- Some beaches are naturist, and others are clothing-optional, so look for signs.
- Bring your own shade and water to the quieter beaches, since facilities are limited.

## The north: Trucadors and the islets

The Trucadors peninsula runs north from the main island towards S'Espalmador. Ses Illetes and Platja de Llevant lie on either side of its narrow spine. The road ends at a car park near the Illetes restaurants, and the last stretch to the northern tip is on foot. The sea here is shallow for a long way out, and the sand is pale. The peninsula is part of Ses Salines d'Eivissa i Formentera Natural Park, which protects the Posidonia meadows and the salt pans.

S'Espalmador is a small uninhabited island, reached by boat or on foot across a narrow, shallow channel when conditions allow. It has a lagoon, a beach called Platja de S'Alga and a mud pool where visitors cover themselves in mud. It is part of the natural park, and rules apply on camping, fires and anchoring.

## The south: Migjorn and Es Arenals

Platja de Migjorn is about five kilometres long. Its western end is near Es Arenals, with a handful of hotels and beach bars. The central part has dunes and a few restaurants, and the eastern end near Es Migjorn is wild. A coastal path runs behind the beach, and cyclists can follow it. The beach faces south and takes the open sea, so it can be rough after a storm.

## The west and south-west

Cala Saona is a sheltered bay with fine sand, a small hotel and restaurants. The coast to the south has low cliffs and rocky ledges, and the lighthouse at Cap de Barbaria is the most southerly point. Cala en Baster, near Es Caló, is a small cove with clear water. Cavall d'en Borràs, a coastal area of rocky ledges, has simple shacks used by local fishermen.

## The east: La Mola

The east end of Formentera rises to the plateau of La Mola, where cliffs plunge into the sea. A path leads down from the plateau to small coves such as Cala en Baster and Es Caló de Sant Agustí. The beach at Es Caló is small but sheltered, and the fishing village still has working boat sheds. The Far de la Mola, a lighthouse built in 1861, stands at the end of the plateau.

## Getting to the beaches

The island has a bus network that links La Savina, Sant Francesc, Es Pujols, Es Pilar de la Mola and several beaches, though a bike is often the quickest way. The cycle path runs from La Savina to Ses Illetes, and from Es Pujols to Migjorn. Hire shops at La Savina rent bikes by the hour and the day.

## Boat trips and snorkelling

Boats from La Savina and Ibiza go to S'Espalmador and Ses Illetes, and some trips stop for snorkelling in the clear water of the Trucadors. Kayaks and paddleboards can be rented at Es Pujols and Migjorn. Snorkelling is good along the rocky shores of Cala Saona and Cap de Barbaria.

## Rules to protect the island

Posidonia seagrass covers much of the seabed, and anchoring on it is prohibited. Dunes are protected, and walking on them is forbidden. Driving vehicles on the beach and camping outside official sites are banned. Visitors who bring cars or motorbikes by ferry face a summer quota. Naturism is common on some beaches, and photography should be discreet.

## When to go

- **Early morning and late afternoon**: Illetes, Llevant and Migjorn have fewer people.
- **June and September**: warm water and fewer crowds.
- **Winter**: the beaches are empty and good for walks, but few services are open.

## Official information

The Consell de Formentera publishes information on beaches, vehicle rules and the natural park. The emergency number is 112.
