Anyone who spends time in the Balearic Islands will see two languages on the road signs, hear two languages in the shops and read two languages on official forms. Catalan and Spanish are both official languages of the islands, and the law gives each of them a defined place. This guide explains what the law says, how it works in daily life and what residents and visitors need to know. It was last checked on 29 October 2026.

## What the law says

The Statute of Autonomy of the Illes Balears, approved as a national organic law in 2007, deals with language in article 4. It says that Catalan, the language proper to the Balearic Islands, is an official language together with Castilian (Spanish). It also says that everyone has the right to know and use both, and that nobody may be discriminated against on the grounds of language. The institutions of the islands must guarantee the normal official use of both languages and take the measures needed to reach full equality between them.

This follows the Spanish Constitution, which makes Castilian the official language of the state and allows other languages to be official in their own communities, as the statutes say. The Balearic Statute names the local variety as Catalan, "the language proper to the Balearic Islands", and in practice it is spoken in distinct island forms. Mallorquí, menorquí and eivissenc are the varieties of Mallorca, Menorca and Ibiza, and Formentera has its own accents. Speakers and writers generally use a common standard in formal contexts.

An earlier regional law, the Ley de Normalización Lingüística of 1986, set out how the language is used in administration, education and the media. The detail of how the law works in schools has changed with different governments, and it remains a topic of political debate. This article does not take a side. It explains the system as set by the Statute and refers readers to the regional government for the current rules.

## In public administration

You can address the Govern, the island councils and the town halls in either language, and they must reply in the language you chose. Official bulletins such as the Butlletí Oficial de les Illes Balears publish in both Catalan and Spanish. Forms, websites and signs are normally bilingual. Public employees are expected to know both languages in many posts, and the regional government runs certified language courses and exams for staff.

In courts, you have the right to use either language, and interpreters are provided for people who do not speak Spanish or Catalan.

## In schools

The regional government decides the language of instruction within the framework of national education law. Children study both Catalan and Spanish as subjects, and the proportion of other subjects taught in each language depends on the school and the regional rules in force. Foreign families who are moving to the islands often ask which language their children will study in. The Conselleria d'Educació publishes the rules each year, and schools can explain them. International schools, which are private, follow their own languages and curricula, and many are in Mallorca, Ibiza and Menorca.

## In everyday life

Most residents speak both languages. In Palma and the main tourist centres, people switch easily between Spanish, Catalan and often English or German as well. In smaller villages and in the interior of Mallorca and Menorca, Catalan is more often the language of everyday conversation. Some people have Spanish as their first language, particularly in towns with a large number of residents from mainland Spain or Latin America.

For visitors, English is widely understood in hotels, restaurants and beach areas. A few phrases in either language are noticed and appreciated:

- **Bon dia** (Catalan) or **buenos días** (Spanish): good morning.
- **Gràcies** or **gracias**: thank you.
- **Si us plau** or **por favor**: please.
- **Adéu** or **adiós**: goodbye.

Street names and place names are officially in Catalan in most places, so you will see Eivissa rather than Ibiza, Maó rather than Mahón, Sóller, Sant Antoni de Portmany and so on. Maps and transport timetables may use either version. Both are correct in different contexts, and you will meet both on road signs.

## For new residents and workers

If you plan to live and work in the islands, a basic knowledge of Spanish is more useful for day to day administration, and a basic knowledge of Catalan is a plus in many jobs, especially in the public sector, teaching and healthcare. Some public jobs require a certificate in Catalan. Language classes are offered by the Govern's Direcció General de Política Lingüística, the Escola Oficial d'Idiomes in Palma and other towns, and private academies.

If you are applying for citizenship or a long-term residence permit, national rules require a Spanish language test, called the DELE A2 for citizenship, and a knowledge test of Spanish culture, called the CCSE. Catalan is not part of those national tests.

## Where to complain or get advice

The Govern runs an office that handles complaints about language rights, the Oficina de Defensa dels Drets Lingüístics. It deals with cases where a person feels they were not able to use one of the official languages with an administration. Its website explains the process.

## Questions people ask

**Do I have to speak Catalan to live in the Balearics?** No. The Statute gives everyone the right to know and use both languages, and Spanish is understood everywhere. Catalan is useful and in some public jobs it is required.

**Which language do officials use with me?** You can choose either official language when you deal with the administration, and the administration must reply in the language you chose.

**Are signs always in both languages?** Many official signs are, but local practice varies. Place names are officially in Catalan in most cases.

**Can I take my driving or citizenship tests in Catalan?** The pages consulted do not say. National tests are administered in Spanish, so check with the relevant authority.
