The Balearic Islands are expected to gain around 200,000 residents over the next 15 years, according to projections from Spain's National Statistics Institute (INE). Pere Salvà, a professor of human geography at the University of the Balearic Islands who has studied the islands' population for decades, considers the forecast realistic — and warns that without planning it could become one of the region's greatest challenges.

## A steady trend

The islands have already added hundreds of thousands of residents since 2000. Earlier INE projections put the population at around 1.4 million by 2037; the latest place that figure in 2041. The rate has eased slightly, but the direction is clear.

## Growth through migration

Practically all of the increase comes from people moving to the islands. Natural growth — births minus deaths — is close to zero.

- Most newcomers arrive to work.
- A smaller share settle as residents without working, such as retirees from other European countries.
- Arrivals from mainland Spain have fallen sharply; today most come from Latin America, especially Colombia and Argentina, and from Morocco, with smaller numbers from countries such as Germany.

Salvà points out that many islanders are themselves the children of earlier waves of migration.

## Why people come

The main draw is work. The economy, built around tourism, depends on a large workforce for jobs in hospitality, construction and services. At the same time the local population is ageing, leaving gaps that employers fill by recruiting from elsewhere.

## Housing: the biggest pressure

Salvà estimates the islands need around 100,000 more homes to house current and future residents — roughly 6,000 a year. When rents are similar to many monthly wages, he warns, people end up spending almost all their income on housing. Without action, that leads to overcrowding and substandard living conditions — the "slums" of the warning.

## Other pressures

More people also means more demand for water and energy, more waste and more traffic on islands where space is limited.

## What could be done

Salvà argues that the current tourism-driven model is reaching its limits. He points to:

- building affordable, energy-efficient homes, especially social housing;
- improving public transport to reduce car dependence;
- managing tourism within the islands' resources;
- encouraging economic activity beyond low-wage service jobs.

Handled well, he says, population growth can keep the economy running and enrich island life. Handled badly, it risks lowering the quality of life for everyone.
