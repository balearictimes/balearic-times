Walk through almost any Balearic town and you will see them: glass-walled padel courts behind a sports centre, and a pilates studio next to a bakery. Both activities have become part of everyday fitness in the islands, for residents and for visitors who stay longer. This article explains what they are, how to start, what to look for in a club or studio and what the official statistics say about their popularity. It makes one point clear at the outset: no official source ranks pilates against yoga or padel against tennis for the Balearics, so this article does not claim that one has replaced the other. It was last checked on 29 October 2026.

## What the official figures do and do not show

Spain's sports council, the Consejo Superior de Deportes (CSD), publishes the number of federation licences held in each sport and each year. Its figures show how many people are licensed to compete or play in federation structures, including padel, tennis and other sports. The CSD also publishes a history of licences and has announced milestones such as Spain passing four million federated athletes. The Ministry of Culture and Sport publishes surveys of sporting habits that ask people what they do.

The limits of these sources matter. Licences count only people who register with a federation, which is a small part of those who play. Many players use public courts or private clubs without a licence. Classes in pilates and yoga are mostly offered by private studios and gyms and are not registered in federation statistics in the same way. For the Balearics specifically, the official data do not provide a ranking of activities by number of practitioners. Readers who want the numbers for padel and tennis licences can look at the CSD's licence tables, and readers who want habits can consult the sports habits survey.

## Padel: the basics

Padel is a racket sport played in doubles on a court about a third the size of a tennis court. The court is enclosed by walls of glass and mesh, and the ball can be played off the walls after it bounces. The rackets are solid, without strings. Serves are underarm. The scoring follows tennis, with points, games and sets. The enclosed court keeps the ball in play, and games are social and fast.

### How to start

- **Find a court.** Many sports centres, clubs and some hotels have padel courts, which you book by the hour. Town halls also run public facilities. Ask at the local sports department.
- **Take a lesson.** A qualified coach can teach the basic strokes and wall play in a few sessions.
- **Rent equipment first.** Most clubs rent rackets and sell balls. Padel shoes with a grip pattern help on artificial turf.
- **Start with a mixed-ability group.** Clubs often organise beginner games and leagues.

### Health points

Padel is a moderate to high-intensity activity with sudden starts and stops. Common problems include sprains, strains of the calf and thigh, and overuse of the elbow and wrist. Warm up, stretch, wear proper shoes and avoid playing in the midday heat in summer. Outdoor courts can be very hot from June to September, and the guidance in the article on the sun applies. Drink water between games.

## Pilates: the basics

Pilates is a system of controlled exercises on a mat or on specialised equipment, aimed at core strength, posture, flexibility and body awareness. It was developed in the early twentieth century by Joseph Pilates. Classes range from gentle mat sessions to equipment-based work with spring-loaded machines, and they suit a wide range of ages and fitness levels.

### How to start

- **Choose a studio.** Look for a studio with small classes, so that the instructor can correct your technique.
- **Ask about qualifications.** Instructors should be able to explain their training. Spain regulates the sports professions, and qualified teachers often hold sports science degrees or recognised pilates certifications.
- **Tell the instructor about injuries.** If you have back pain, are pregnant or have had surgery, tell the instructor and ask your doctor first.
- **Try a trial class.** Many studios offer one.

### Health points

Pilates is low impact and is often recommended as part of a programme for general fitness and back care, but it is not a treatment on its own. See a doctor for persistent pain. Poor technique can cause strain, so go slowly and ask for adjustments.

## Why they fit island life

Neither activity needs a lot of space or a large group. Padel courts fit in small plots. Studios can operate in a small room. Both work year-round, indoors or outdoors, and both are social. In a place with strong seasonal swings in the population, activities that welcome newcomers and offer quick access to a group make it easier to meet people. Hotels and sports centres also use them to draw guests in the shoulder seasons.

## Cost and booking

This article does not give prices, because no official price list exists and fees vary by club, island and season. Compare several places, ask what is included and check cancellation rules.

## Safety and insurance

Check whether your club or studio has liability insurance and first-aid equipment. If you play in a league, ask whether a sports licence with insurance is required. Travel insurance may not cover sports injuries, so read the policy.

## Other options

If padel or pilates are not for you, the islands offer many alternatives: walking, cycling, swimming, tennis, yoga and gym classes. The article on outdoor fitness describes routes and rules.

## Questions people ask

**Do I need to be fit to start padel?** No. Beginners can play at a gentle pace, and the enclosed court helps keep the ball in play.

**Is pilates only for people with back problems?** No. It is used for general strength, posture and flexibility. Tell the instructor about any condition.

**Can visitors join classes?** Most studios and clubs accept drop-in visitors or sell short packs. Ask in advance in the high season.
