For more than four decades, Julian Barnes has been one of the most admired voices in English literature. His readers in Spain know him well: he has visited Barcelona on several occasions, taking part in public conversations at La Pedrera and at the Centre de Cultura Contemporània, where audiences queued to have their books signed.

## From Metroland onwards

Barnes's first novel, *Metroland* (1980), follows two friends from their rebellious adolescence in the London suburbs to a settled adulthood. Already present are the features that would define his work: humour, a touch of cynicism, and a sharp eye for the gap between who we want to be and who we become.

## Memory as invention

Memory is perhaps his great subject. In *Flaubert's Parrot* (1984), one of his best-known books, he plays with biography and fiction to show how hard it is to know the truth about anyone's life. In *The Sense of an Ending*, which won the Booker Prize in 2011, a man in late middle age discovers that his memories of his youth are not what he thought. For Barnes, memory is closer to imagination than to an accurate record — a reminder that every account of the past is an interpretation.

## Love and suffering

*The Only Story* (2018) opens with a question that sums up its theme: is it better to love more and suffer more, or to love less and suffer less? The novel follows a young man's relationship with an older married woman and its long consequences.

## Facing death

In *Nothing to Be Frightened Of* (2008), Barnes, a self-declared agnostic, examines his fear of death and his relationship with the idea of God, mixing family memories, philosophy and dark humour. It is one of his most personal and most quoted books.

## A writer engaged with the world

Barnes treats each book as a new challenge, refusing to repeat a successful formula. He has also spoken out on public issues, and has been a sharp critic of Brexit, which he has described as a self-inflicted wound for his country.

## Truth and accuracy

What unites his work is a belief that the writer's task is to tell the truth about life as accurately as possible — not through grand statements, but through precise observation of how people think, love, remember and deceive themselves. His clear, elegant prose can move from comedy to melancholy in a single paragraph.

For readers discovering him, *The Sense of an Ending* is a good starting point: short, gripping and full of the questions that have occupied him throughout his career.
