Menorca is the quietest of the three larger Balearic Islands, and its coastline is a big reason why. The whole island has been a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve since 1993, and development near the sea has been limited. The result is a coast of pine-backed coves, clear water and few high-rise hotels.

## The south coast: white sand and turquoise water

The south has the beaches that appear on postcards. Cala Macarella is a curved bay of pale sand and pine trees, and its smaller neighbour Macarelleta is reached by a path round the rocks. A little further on, Cala Turqueta has a similar look, with a bit more room to spread out. Cala Mitjana and Cala Mitjaneta are quieter options between Galdana and Turqueta.

Cala Galdana is a resort beach with a long stretch of sand, restaurants and a gentle slope, which suits families who want facilities nearby.

Son Bou is the longest beach on the island, at roughly two and a half kilometres. It has a good range of sunbeds and cafés, and plenty of space even when it is busy.

## The north: red sand and wilder coast

The north coast is more rugged. Cala Pregonda has red-tinted sand and rocky islets offshore, and the walk from the nearest car park takes about 20 minutes. Playa de Cavalleria and Binimel·là are wide, exposed beaches with fine sand, backed by low hills. The wind can make the north uncomfortable, so check the forecast before you go.

Es Grau, in the east, has a shallow, protected bay, and it sits next to the S'Albufera des Grau natural park. It is calm and a good option for small children.

## The east and south-east

Punta Prima and Binibeca have gentle waters and tidy resorts. Binibeca Vell, nearby, is a whitewashed fishing village style development that attracts photographers. Cala en Porter on the south coast sits in a cliff-sided inlet and is known for the cave bar above it.

## Walking between coves

The Camí de Cavalls footpath runs round the entire island and links many of the coves. You can walk from Macarella to Turqueta in a short time through woodland, which is one of the best ways to see two beaches in a day without moving the car.

## Practical points

- Car parks at the popular south coast beaches fill by mid-morning in July and August, and in some places parking is paid or controlled. Arrive early or take a bus where one runs.
- Many coves have no facilities. Bring water, shade and something to eat.
- Many small coves have no lifeguard, so check the flags where there are any and swim only when conditions are calm.
- Take your rubbish with you. The island works hard to protect its beaches.
- Do not remove sand, stones or seagrass. Posidonia meadows protect the water and the shoreline, and boats are asked not to anchor on them.

## South coast cove by cove

The coves of the south coast are reached from Ciutadella and Cala Galdana along narrow roads, and each has a car park at the top of a path. Cala Macarella and Cala Macarelleta are linked by a footpath cut into the rock. Cala Turqueta lies west of Macarella and can be reached on foot via the Camí de Cavalls in around 30 minutes. Cala Mitjana and Cala Mitjaneta are on the eastern side of Galdana, with pale sand and rocky headlands, and Cala Trebalúger is a narrow inlet reached by a stream path.

Cala Galdana, the largest resort on the south coast, is built around a horseshoe bay with a river mouth at one end. The Camí de Cavalls links it to the nearby cove of Cala Mitjana in around an hour on foot. Further east, small inlets such as Cala Fustam and Cala Escorxada are reached by rough tracks and few visitors get there. Binigaus and Sant Tomàs are longer beaches near Es Migjorn Gran, with dunes and a protected status.

## Son Bou and the central south

Son Bou is the island's longest beach. An early Christian basilica, the Basilica de Son Bou, stands on a rise behind the dunes, and the area has a lagoon that attracts birds. Its eastern end has a nudist section, and hotels line the beach at the other end. The village of Alaior, inland, is known for its cheese factory and its shoe workshops, and the beach is a ten-minute drive away.

## The north coast

Cala Pregonda is reached on foot from the car park near Binimel·là and is marked by red sand and rocky islets. Binimel·là and Cavalleria are wide beaches with dunes in the municipality of Es Mercadal. The road to the Cavalleria lighthouse gives views over the cape. The Menorcan north coast is windier than the south, and its beaches often have fewer people.

Fornells, a harbour village on a deep bay, is well known for its lobster restaurants. A footpath leads up to the Torre de Fornells, a small round watchtower built by the British. Further east, Cala Tirant and Platja de Tirant are lined with dunes and are often quiet. Es Grau, with its calm lagoon, is the gateway to the S'Albufera des Grau natural park.

## The east coast and Mahón

Punta Prima is a small resort with a beach and the islet of Illa de l'Aire, which has a lighthouse. Binibeca Vell, a white village built in the 1970s in the style of a fisherman's hamlet, is on the same coast. Cala Alcaufar and Cala Sant Esteve are close to Mahón. Cala Sant Esteve sits below Fort Marlborough, a British fortress of the 18th century.

## Things to bring

- **Water and food**: there are few shops near the coves.
- **Shade**: pine trees give shade in some places, but not all.
- **Sandals**: avarcas or water shoes help on rocky paths.
- **Cash**: small beach bars sometimes accept only cash.

## Biosphere reserve rules

Menorca's Biosphere Reserve status brings restrictions on building and on sensitive coastal areas. Dogs must be on a lead, and campfires are banned. Do not remove seagrass or sand. Boats should use buoys where provided, and many bays have a limit on the number of boats.

## Getting to the beaches

Local buses link Ciutadella and Mahón with Cala Galdana, Son Bou, Es Grau and Punta Prima in season. In recent summers, bus links to the south coast coves near Macarella have run from Ciutadella and Cala Galdana to save car park space. The Consell Insular de Menorca publishes updates on parking rules and access.

## Official information

The Consell Insular de Menorca and the town halls of Ciutadella, Ferreries, Es Migjorn Gran and Alaior publish beach information. The emergency number is 112.
