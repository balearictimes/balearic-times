Ibiza is better known for its clubs than its kitchens, but the island has a real food tradition built on fish, vegetables, cured meat and almonds. A few dishes are worth looking for, especially away from the beachfront menus with photographs on them.

## Bullit de peix

The best-known dish is bullit de peix, a fish stew made with potatoes, saffron and olive oil. It is served in two parts. First comes the fish and potatoes, often with an alioli, and then the cooking liquid is used to make a rice dish called arròs a banda. Traditional restaurants, many of them on the coast, take pride in doing it properly, and the dish is usually made for two or more people. Ask when you book, as some places need notice.

## Sofrit pagès

This is the winter dish of the countryside. It combines lamb or chicken, local sausages and potatoes in a rich stew, finished with a sprinkling of saffron and sometimes a splash of wine. It appears at village fiestas and Christmas meals, and you will find it on some restaurant menus out of season.

## Ensalada payesa

The farmhouse salad is simple and cheap. Tomato, peppers, onion and potato are tossed with olive oil, and the dish is often topped with dried fish or sliced olives. It makes a good lunch in the heat.

## Flaó and other sweets

Flaó is the island's signature dessert, a cheesecake made with fresh sheep or goat cheese and flavoured with mint and aniseed. It is baked at home for festivals and sold in bakeries. Look as well for orelletes, fried pastries flavoured with aniseed, which are linked to Christmas.

## Hierbas ibicencas

The island's herbal liqueur is made by macerating local herbs in anise. It is served after a meal, often over ice, and the sweetness varies by brand. A small glass is a common way to end lunch.

## Markets and shopping

Local produce is easiest to find at the weekly village markets. Sant Carles and Santa Eulària both have regular stalls selling cheese, olives, honey and fruit. Saturday is a good day to look. The hippy market at Las Dalias is better known for crafts and clothes than for food, but it is a popular stop and has food stalls.

## Eating like a local

- Spanish lunch starts late, often after two o'clock, and dinner rarely begins before half past eight. Restaurants that open at six are usually aimed at tourists.
- Many places offer a set menu at lunch on weekdays. It is usually the best value of the day.
- Beach clubs charge a premium for the setting. If you are watching your budget, eat in town and visit the beach clubs for a drink.

## Where the traditions live

The best way to find the island's food is to leave the beachfront. In Ibiza Town, the old quarter of Dalt Vila has small restaurants and bars along the cobbled streets, and the Mercat Nou, the covered market in the lower town, sells fish, vegetables, cheese and sausages. The Mercat Vell, close to Dalt Vila, is a smaller market with local produce.

On the east coast, Santa Eulària des Riu has a long seafront with a mix of traditional and international restaurants, and the nearby village of Jesús has a small church known for a medieval altarpiece. In the north, Sant Joan de Labritja and Portinatx have fish restaurants close to the sea, while the valley of Santa Agnès de Corona is known for its almond blossom in January and February. The countryside around Santa Gertrudis de Fruitera has a cluster of cafés and restaurants that serve country dishes, and the village is a favourite stop for cyclists.

## The coastal fish places

The south-west coast around Es Cubells and the beach of Cala d'Hort has several simple fish restaurants. Es Cubells is a small village on a cliff with a church that dates from the 18th century. On the east coast, the village of Es Canar hosts a hippy market on Wednesdays in summer, and Cala Llonga has a small selection of beachfront restaurants.

Sant Antoni's harbour area has several restaurants serving fish and rice dishes. Visitors who want a quieter look at the bay can walk along the quay early in the day.

## Wine, spirits and liqueurs

Ibiza has a small wine tradition, and several vineyards around Sant Mateu d'Albarca and Santa Agnès make red and white wines from local grapes. Some of them offer tastings by appointment. The island's herbal liqueur, hierbas ibicencas, is sold in supermarkets and shops across the island. Frígola, a thyme-based liqueur from Formentera and Ibiza, is another local drink, served cold after a meal.

## The seasonal calendar

- **January to March**: almond blossom in the north, and traditional sofrit pagès on winter menus.
- **Spring**: fresh broad beans, artichokes and wild asparagus appear in markets.
- **Summer**: tomatoes, peppers and figs are at their best, and fish stews are served at coastal restaurants.
- **Autumn**: mushrooms, pomegranates and olives appear, and the almond harvest takes place.
- **December**: orelletes, ensaladas and traditional Christmas cakes.

## Fiestas and food

Many village fiestas include a communal meal. The patron saint's days in villages such as Sant Joan de Labritja and Santa Gertrudis are among the best known, with folk dances in the traditional costume called the ball pagès. The costumes, with their silver filigree jewellery, are a recognisable part of Ibizan culture, and the dances are performed at village squares in the summer.

## What to pay

Prices in beachfront restaurants and beach clubs are often twice those in inland villages. Expect to pay more for a fish stew meant for two, and ask whether the price is by weight for any whole fish. A set lunch menu in a village restaurant is generally the cheapest way to try local dishes.

## Dietary notes

Vegetarian options are widely available in tourist areas, especially in Ibiza Town, Santa Eulària and Sant Antoni. The island has a long tradition of health-focused cafés, and gluten-free and vegan menus are common. Traditional dishes use meat and fish heavily, so ask for details if you have an allergy.
