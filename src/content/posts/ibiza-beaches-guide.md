Ibiza has more than fifty beaches and coves, and they are very different from one another. Some are wide and busy with sunbeds and beach clubs. Others are a few metres of pebbles at the foot of a pine-covered cliff. Choosing well depends on what you want from the day.

## The west coast for sunsets

The west is where people go to watch the sun go down. Cala Comte, on the coast of Sant Josep, has clear shallow water, rocky platforms and a few beach bars. It fills up well before sunset in July and August, so arrive early if you want a place to sit.

Cala Bassa is nearby and has sand, sunbeds and a gentle slope into the water, which suits families. Cala Salada and its smaller neighbour Cala Saladeta sit in a sheltered inlet near Sant Antoni. Saladeta is reached on foot down a short path, which keeps the crowds a little thinner.

## The south for space

The south of the island has the longest stretches of sand. Ses Salines, inside the natural park of the same name, is a wide beach backed by dunes and salt flats. Its eastern end, Es Cavallet, is known as a naturist section and is also popular with people who simply want room to spread out.

Platja d'en Bossa is the longest beach close to the airport and Ibiza Town. It is lively, loud in summer and lined with hotels and clubs. It is not the quiet option, but it is convenient.

## The north and east for calm

Benirràs, on the north coast, has a steep bay and a cluster of beach shacks. It is known for its Sunday evening drumming at sunset, a habit that began decades ago and continues. Cala Xarraca, a short drive away, is calmer and good for snorkelling.

On the east side, Santa Eulària has a town beach with a promenade, which makes it an easy choice for people without a car. Cala Llonga is a sheltered bay with soft sand and calm water.

## Looking at Es Vedra

Cala d'Hort, in the south-west, is the best-known place to see Es Vedra, the rock island that rises about 380 metres from the sea. The beach itself is pebbly and small, but the view is the reason to go. Local lore attaches plenty of myths to the rock, from magnetic fields to sirens. None of that is proven, but the sight is worth the journey.

## Practical points

- Parking near the most popular coves fills early in summer. Some town councils run seasonal bus links to the busiest beaches, so check before you hire a car.
- Many beaches have no shade or facilities. Take water, a hat and something to eat.
- Dropping anchor on seagrass is banned around the island, because the Posidonia meadows protect the water quality. If you hire a boat, ask the operator where you can anchor.
- The best months for swimming are June to September, when the sea is warm. In October the water is still pleasant and the beaches are far emptier.

## Beach by beach on the west and south-west

Cala Comte, sometimes written Cala Conta, lies on the coast of Sant Josep de sa Talaia, about 15 minutes by road from Sant Antoni. The small islands offshore, including Illa des Bosc, make the water shallow and very clear, and the rocky platforms either side of the sand are where people sit for the sunset. A few beach bars operate in summer, and the car park fills by late morning.

Further south, Cala Tarida is a wider bay with a gentle slope, facing west and lined with a few restaurants. Cala Vedella is a short drive beyond it and has a protected, horseshoe-shaped bay that suits families. Between Cala Vedella and Cala d'Hort the road climbs towards the Torre des Savinar, a coastal watchtower that gives the best land view of Es Vedra and a favourite spot for the sunset.

Cala d'Hort itself has two small restaurants on the sand. The beach is pebbly, and the main reason to stop is the view of Es Vedra and its neighbour, the smaller rock of Es Vedranell. Nearby, the disused Sa Pedrera quarry, locally called Atlantis, draws curious walkers, but the cliff edges are unprotected and the sea can be dangerous, so take care.

## The south coast and Ses Salines

Ses Salines Natural Park covers the salt pans and dunes at the southern tip of the island and extends under the sea to the north of Formentera. The beach at Ses Salines is long and sandy, and the chiringuito Sa Trinxa is one of the best-known beach bars on the island, with music and a casual atmosphere. Es Cavallet, at the eastern end, is the main naturist stretch, backed by sand dunes and juniper.

The salt pans themselves are still worked in some places, and the park is a good place for birdwatching in spring and autumn. Flamingos stop here on migration. Close to the pans, the Sa Caleta archaeological site holds the remains of a Phoenician settlement founded around the 7th century BC. It is part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing that Ibiza received in 1999, which also covers the old town of Dalt Vila, the Puig des Molins necropolis and the seagrass meadows of Posidonia that grow off the south coast.

Cala Jondal, a few minutes west of Ses Salines, is a pebble beach known for its upmarket beach clubs such as Blue Marlin. It is one of the more expensive stretches of coast, and beds and tables are often booked in advance.

## The north and the east

Cala Xarraca is a small cove in a bay close to Portinatx, with a wooded slope behind it and calm water good for snorkelling. Portinatx itself has several small beaches within a short walk of each other, and it sits near the lighthouse at Punta des Moscarter, which looks out over open sea.

On the east coast, Aigües Blanques, whose name means white waters, is known for its pale clay cliffs. Clothing is optional on part of the beach, and the clay is often used for natural face masks. Cala Boix, a dark pebble beach near Es Figueral, is quieter, and the water is clear. Cala Nova and Es Figueral have soft sand and tend to attract families.

Santa Eulària des Riu, the main town on the east side, has a promenade lined with restaurants and a small beach beside the marina. It makes a good base for visitors without a car, since the bus network reaches several beaches from there.

## Choosing between them

- **For sunsets**: Cala Comte, Cala d'Hort and the cafés on the Sant Antoni seafront.
- **For clear shallow water with children**: Cala Bassa, Es Figueral and Talamanca.
- **For space and dunes**: Ses Salines and Es Cavallet.
- **For a quieter day**: Cala Xarraca, Cala Boix and the small coves around Portinatx.
- **For beach clubs**: Cala Jondal and Platja d'en Bossa.

## Official information

Local councils post beach flag colours, lifeguard hours and any seasonal access rules on their own websites. For safety warnings and the latest advice, the Spanish emergency number 112 operates across the islands, and the Ibiza island council, the Consell d'Eivissa, publishes updates on traffic and the summer vehicle limits on its website.
