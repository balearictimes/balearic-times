Millions of British visitors travel to the Balearics each year, and most trips go smoothly. A few checks before you leave and some local habits to know about will make them smoother still.

## Documents

Spain is in the Schengen Area, and UK visitors need a valid passport. The rules about passport validity and the length of stay are set by the Schengen system, and new border and registration systems for non-EU travellers have been phased in. Check the latest guidance on the UK government's foreign travel advice pages before you go, as the rules have been changing.

## Health cover

A UK Global Health Insurance Card, the GHIC, gives you access to state-provided healthcare in Spain on the same terms as residents. It does not replace travel insurance, which should cover private treatment, repatriation, lost luggage and cancellation. If you plan to hire a scooter, do water sports or hike in the mountains, check that your policy includes these.

In an emergency, call 112. Pharmacies, marked with a green cross, can advise on minor problems, and many staff speak English in tourist areas.

## Money

The euro is used across Spain. Cards are widely accepted in hotels, restaurants and shops, though small bars and market stalls may prefer cash. Cash machines are easy to find in towns. When paying by card, choose to be charged in euros if you are offered the option, since the conversion rate offered at the till is usually worse. Tipping is modest. Leaving a euro or two, or rounding up the bill, is common in restaurants, but service is not added automatically.

## Time and daily rhythm

Spain is an hour ahead of the UK. Shops in smaller towns often close for a break in the afternoon, and lunch is eaten late. Dinner starts after half past eight in most restaurants. Plan your day around the heat, with the beach in the morning, a long lunch and a rest, and an outing in the evening.

## Plugs and phones

Spanish sockets take the two-pin continental plug, so bring an adaptor. Mobile roaming charges depend on your UK provider, and many have introduced fees since Brexit, so check your plan or use Wi-Fi.

## Sun, heat and sea

- Use high-factor sunscreen, and reapply it after swimming.
- Drink water through the day, and avoid strenuous activity at midday in July and August.
- Jellyfish appear in the sea in some summers. Local lifeguards post warnings, and pharmacies sell treatments for stings.
- Check the beach flags. Green means safe, yellow means take care, and red means no swimming.

## Safety and scams

Keep an eye on bags in crowded places, such as markets, airports and busy beaches. Do not leave valuables in a parked car. Use licensed taxis, and agree the fare or insist on the meter. Be careful with scooter hire, and inspect the vehicle before you sign.

## Language

Catalan, in its Balearic form, is the local language, alongside Spanish. English is widely understood in tourist areas. A few words such as "bon dia" (good morning) and "gràcies" (thank you) go down well.

## Before you go

- Check your passport and the latest entry rules.
- Pack your GHIC and travel insurance details.
- Book transfers, hire cars and restaurants in advance for July and August.
- Check your hotel for the tourist tax and any local rules.

## Before you travel: the checks that matter

Look at the UK government's foreign travel advice for Spain on gov.uk before each trip. It covers passport validity, entry requirements for UK citizens, the length of stay allowed in the Schengen Area, and any new border systems. Rules for non-EU visitors, including biometric registration and travel authorisation schemes, have been changing in recent years, so do not rely on advice from previous trips.

Check that your passport is in good condition and has enough validity. Airlines may refuse boarding if your passport does not meet the rules. If you are travelling with children, carry their documents, and a letter of consent if you are travelling with only one parent.

## Health care in practice

The Balearic public health service, IB-Salut, runs health centres and hospitals. The main hospital in Palma is Son Espases, and Ibiza has Can Misses, while Menorca has Mateu Orfila in Mahón. Formentera has a health centre in Sant Francesc. If you need urgent care, go to the nearest emergency department, called urgencias, or call 112. A GHIC covers state care on the same terms as residents, but you may still need to pay for some services and recover costs later.

Pharmacies, marked with a green cross, take turns to stay open at night. A list of duty pharmacies is posted on the door of each pharmacy. Staff can help with minor problems, such as sunburn, stings and stomach upsets, and sell many medicines without a prescription.

## Insurance

Take out travel insurance that covers medical treatment, repatriation, cancellation and baggage. If you plan to ride a scooter, check that the policy covers you for the engine size and requires a licence. Water sports, hiking in the mountains and sailing may need extra cover. Keep a copy of the policy number and emergency contact in your phone.

## Money and costs

The euro is the currency. Cash machines are plentiful in towns, but quieter beaches and villages may have none. Contactless payment is common. Some small bars, markets and taxis take only cash. Check the exchange rate, and avoid dynamic currency conversion, which lets the merchant convert your payment to pounds at a poor rate.

Tipping is not compulsory, but rounding up a bill by a euro or two is appreciated. In restaurants, a service charge is not normally added. Taxi drivers appreciate small tips, and hotel staff are tipped as in the UK.

## Language and culture

Catalan is the first language of many locals, and Spanish is widely understood. English is common in tourist areas and hotels. Greetings matter: say "bon dia" or "buenos días" when you enter a shop. Mealtimes are later than in the UK, with lunch from two and dinner from nine in many places. Shops often close for a break in the afternoon, and many are shut on Sundays outside tourist areas.

## Phones and power

Check your mobile roaming terms before you travel, since many UK providers charge for use in the EU. Wi-Fi is common in hotels and cafés. Spanish sockets have two round pins, so bring an adaptor. The voltage is 230 volts, which is the same as the UK.

## Sun, heat and sea safety

The sun is strong from May to September. Use sunscreen with a high factor, wear a hat and sunglasses, and take shade at midday. Drink water often. In heatwaves, the regional authorities issue warnings and recommend staying indoors in the afternoon. Read the beach flags. Green means safe, yellow means take care and red means do not swim.

## Getting help

- **Emergency**: 112, available in English.
- **Police**: Policia Local, Policia Nacional and Guardia Civil.
- **Lost passport**: contact the UK embassy in Madrid or the nearest consulate through the gov.uk website.
- **Lost bank cards**: call your bank's international number.

## A short packing list

- Passport, GHIC and insurance documents
- Euros in cash for small purchases
- A plug adaptor
- Sun cream, a hat and sandals for rocky coves
- A light jacket for evenings in spring and autumn
- A copy of your booking confirmations

## Official information

The UK government's foreign travel advice for Spain is on gov.uk. The Spanish tourist board publishes visitor information at spain.info, and the Balearic government covers health and emergency services at caib.es.
