The Balearic summer is long, bright and hot. That is why people come, and it is also why every year some visitors and residents end up in a health centre with sunburn, heat exhaustion or a sea injury. Most of these problems are preventable. This guide collects the standard advice that Spanish health and weather authorities give, and explains where to find the official warnings. It is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take regular medicines, ask your doctor before travelling. It was last checked on 29 October 2026.

## Know the official warnings

Spain's state meteorological agency, AEMET, publishes weather warnings for each area at aemet.es and in its app. For heat, the warnings are colour coded as yellow, orange and red, with the colour depending on the temperature thresholds set for each zone. The Balearic Islands are divided into zones, so check the one where you are staying. The Ministry of Health publishes a national plan of preventive actions against the effects of excess heat, and the regional government issues advice during heatwaves.

When an orange or red warning is in force, avoid strenuous activity outdoors in the middle of the day, stay in cool places and check on older neighbours and relatives.

## Protect your skin

The advice from health authorities is consistent:

- Use a broad-spectrum sunscreen with a high protection factor, and reapply it every two hours and after swimming.
- Avoid direct sun in the middle of the day, when the sun is strongest. Seek shade, wear a hat and sunglasses and cover your shoulders.
- Be extra careful with children and babies. Babies under a year old should be kept out of direct sun.
- Remember that sea breezes and cloud can hide the heat. Burn risk remains.
- People with fair skin or who take medicines that increase sensitivity to light should take additional care.

If you burn, move out of the sun, cool the skin with water, drink fluids and seek advice from a pharmacist. Blistering over a large area, fever, or burns in a child need a doctor.

## Drink enough

In hot weather, drink water regularly before you feel thirsty. Alcohol, coffee and sugary drinks do not replace water. Eat light meals and fruit. Older people, children and people with chronic illness are most at risk of dehydration.

## Recognise heat illness

Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache and nausea. Move the person to a cool place, loosen clothing, give water in small sips and cool the body with damp cloths. Heat stroke is an emergency: the person may have very high body temperature, hot dry skin, confusion or loss of consciousness. Call 112 immediately and cool the person while you wait.

## The beach flag system

Beaches in the islands use a flag system, which is standard across Spain:

- **Green:** safe to swim.
- **Yellow:** take care, because conditions are changing.
- **Red:** do not swim.

Lifeguards operate on many beaches in summer, but not all beaches have them, and not all hours are covered. Swim where lifeguards are on duty and never swim alone or after drinking alcohol. Watch children at all times near water. Sea conditions can change quickly, especially on exposed beaches in wind.

## Jellyfish and sea life

Jellyfish appear in some summers, and beaches may post warnings. If you are stung, leave the water, rinse the area with seawater rather than fresh water and avoid rubbing. Pharmacies sell treatments, and a doctor should look at severe reactions or stings in children. Sea urchins on rocky coves can cause painful injuries, so wear water shoes.

## Protect nature when you protect yourself

Fire risk is high in summer. Do not light fires, and put out cigarettes carefully. Authorities restrict fires in natural areas, and fines can be heavy. Do not take sand, stones or seagrass from beaches.

## Walking and cycling in the heat

If you hike in the Serra de Tramuntana or cycle on the Mallorca roads, start early, carry plenty of water, wear a hat and tell someone your route. Mobile coverage can be poor in the mountains. Turn back if you feel unwell.

## Food and water

Ask your accommodation whether the tap water is suitable for drinking, because the answer differs from place to place, and buy bottled water if you are told to. Keep food cool in hot weather, and avoid leaving it in a car.

## If you need help

- Call 112 in an emergency.
- Go to a health centre or the emergency department for urgent problems.
- Ask at a pharmacy for advice on minor problems.
- Keep your health card (GHIC or EHIC) and insurance details to hand.

## Who needs extra care

Some people are more at risk in hot weather, and health authorities single them out:

- **Babies and young children.** They overheat quickly and cannot tell you they are unwell. Keep them in shade, dress them in light clothing and offer fluids often.
- **Older people.** The body's ability to cool down declines with age, and some medicines make heat harder to cope with. Check on older relatives and neighbours during heatwaves.
- **People with long-term conditions.** Heart, kidney and respiratory conditions can worsen in the heat. Ask your doctor how to adjust medicines and fluids.
- **Outdoor workers.** Employers have duties to protect staff from heat, including breaks and water. Hospitality and construction workers in the islands are exposed in July and August.
- **Pregnant women.** Heat and dehydration carry extra risks, so rest in the shade and drink regularly.

## Questions people ask

**Can I get burned on a cloudy day?** Yes. Ultraviolet radiation passes through thin cloud, and the sea breeze hides the heat on your skin.

**When is the sun strongest?** In the middle of the day, which is why health advice says to seek shade then and do outdoor exercise early or late.

**Should I put sunscreen on children under six months?** Babies that young should be kept out of direct sun. Ask a pharmacist or paediatrician for advice.

**What if I feel unwell at the beach?** Tell a lifeguard if one is on duty, move to shade and drink water. Call 112 if the person is confused, faint or unresponsive.
