Moving to an island, working long hours in a season, living far from family and finding somewhere to live can all put pressure on a person's mental health. These pressures are common, and getting help early makes a difference. This guide explains what official support exists in the Balearic Islands and in Spain, and how to reach it. It is not a substitute for professional care. If you or someone near you is in immediate danger, call 112. It was last checked on 29 October 2026.

## If you need help now

- **In an emergency, call 112.** The line is free, works across the islands and has operators who can handle calls in English.
- **Call 024.** This is the national helpline for people who are thinking about suicide, and for their families and friends. It is run by the Ministry of Health, it is free, and it is available at any hour of the day. Staff are trained to listen and to help you find the next step.
- **Go to an emergency department.** Any public hospital can see you in a crisis.

If you are worried about someone else, stay with them if you can, speak calmly and encourage them to contact one of these services.

## The public system

Mental health care in the islands is part of IB-Salut, the public health service. The usual route is through your family doctor at your health centre, who can assess you, treat common conditions such as anxiety and depression, and refer you to specialist mental health teams if needed. To use the service you need to be covered by the public system, which the article on how IB-Salut works explains. Residents who are not covered can sign the special agreement and pay the monthly fee, which IB-Salut sets at 60 euros for those under 65 and 157 euros for those aged 65 and over. Everyone in an emergency can be seen at urgencias.

Waiting times for specialist care can be long in any health system, so ask your doctor what to expect and whether short-term support is available.

## Private support

Private psychologists and therapists work in Palma and the other main towns, and some offer sessions in English and other languages. Fees are set by each practitioner, and there is no official price list. Check that the person is registered with the professional college of psychologists or the medical college, and ask about their training. Some travel insurance and private health plans cover therapy, so read your policy.

## Pressures that are common in the islands

The following situations come up often, and none of them is a sign of weakness:

- **Loneliness.** Many residents and workers are far from family and friends, and winter is quiet.
- **Seasonal insecurity.** Work that ends in October can create worry about money and housing.
- **Housing stress.** Finding and keeping a home is hard. The article on housing explains the measures being taken.
- **Long hours and exhaustion.** Hospitality work in summer can mean split shifts and little rest.
- **Alcohol.** In party areas, drinking can become a habit that affects sleep, mood and work.
- **Language barriers.** Not speaking the language makes it harder to ask for help.

If any of these is affecting you for more than a couple of weeks, speak to a doctor.

## Small steps that help

These are general suggestions that health services commonly give, not treatment:

- Keep a regular sleep routine where your shifts allow it.
- Stay in contact with someone you trust, even by phone.
- Spend time outdoors. The article on outdoor fitness lists walking and swimming options.
- Join a club, class or community group to meet people.
- Watch your alcohol intake, and ask for help if you feel you cannot cut down.
- Ask for help early rather than waiting for a crisis.

## Supporting a colleague or friend

Notice changes in behaviour, such as withdrawal, missed shifts, heavy drinking or talk of hopelessness. Ask directly and kindly how they are. Listen without judging. Offer to go with them to a health centre. If they talk about suicide, take it seriously, stay with them and call 112 or 024.

## For employers

Employers have duties to protect workers' health, including psychosocial risks. Managers can help by making schedules predictable where possible, allowing breaks, knowing how to direct staff to help and treating requests for time off for health reasons with respect. The Balearic labour authority and the national labour inspectorate publish guidance on occupational risk prevention.

## Language support

The article on languages explains that you have the right to use either official language with the administration. In health settings, ask whether an interpreter is available, and bring someone you trust if you need to. Some community and church groups for foreign residents offer informal support, and your consulate or embassy can give you information on local services in an emergency.

## Where to look for more

- 024 and the Ministry of Health's pages (sanidad.gob.es).
- IB-Salut for your local health centre and services (ibsalut.es).
- Your town hall's social services department, which can advise on benefits and local resources.

## Questions people ask

**Will asking for help affect my residence or work?** Seeing a doctor about your mental health is a medical matter. Ask the health centre how your information is protected, and remember that you are entitled to confidentiality in medical care.

**What if I am not covered by the public system?** You can use emergency care at any hospital. For continuing care, consider the special agreement with IB-Salut or private cover, as the articles on healthcare explain.

**What if I do not speak Spanish well enough?** Bring someone you trust, write down what you want to say and ask whether an interpreter or a staff member who speaks your language is available.

**Is it normal to feel low after moving?** Many people find the first months hard. If the low mood lasts more than a couple of weeks or affects sleep, work or appetite, speak to a doctor.
