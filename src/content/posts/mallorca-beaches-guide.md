Mallorca has over 200 beaches and coves, and they range from long family bays with lifeguards and sunbeds to rocky inlets reached by a footpath. The island is large enough that the coasts feel quite different, so it helps to think by area.

## The south-east: coves and clear water

The area around Santanyí has some of the island's best-known coves. Caló des Moro is a tiny inlet between steep cliffs, reached by a short path from the road. The sand is limited and the beach is very busy in summer, so many people go early in the morning or late in the day. Do not expect facilities.

Cala Mondragó is part of a natural park and has two sandy bays, pine woods and a small café. Cala Llombards and Cala Santanyí are further options with white sand and calm water.

## The south: Es Trenc

Es Trenc is the long, wild beach near Campos that visitors often compare with the Caribbean. It has fine pale sand, shallow turquoise water and dunes behind it. It is part of a protected area, and there are a few beach bars at the Ses Covetes end. Parking can be crowded in July and August. A large protest took place there in 2026 against plans seen as a threat to protected coast, which shows how much the beach matters to residents.

## The north: wide bays for families

Around Alcúdia and Muro, the bay of Alcúdia has long stretches of fine sand and very gentle water, which suits small children and less confident swimmers. Platja de Muro is backed by dunes and pine woods and is one of the best-equipped beaches in the north.

Formentor beach, near Port de Pollença, has a pine-fringed setting and clear water. In summer, access to the road beyond it is restricted during the day, and the beach car park fills quickly.

## The north-east: Cala Agulla and Cala Ratjada

Cala Agulla, near Capdepera, is a large bay with soft sand, sunbeds and a nature reserve behind it. Cala Ratjada, a short distance away, has several smaller beaches, a harbour and a busy centre.

## The west: coves under the mountains

The west coast is steeper. Cala Tuent and Sa Calobra are reached by narrow, winding roads from the Tramuntana, and the drive is part of the experience. Deià has a pebble cove with a few simple restaurants. Port de Sóller has a small town beach and a tram connecting it with Sóller.

## Beaches that need a walk

Cala Varques, near Manacor, is a pretty cove of white sand and cliffs reached on foot, with no facilities nearby. Allow time for the walk and carry water. Such coves are worth the effort for people who want to avoid the crowds.

## Practical points

- In July and August, aim to be at the popular coves by about nine in the morning. Many car parks fill soon after.
- Check the flag colours at the beach. Red means no swimming.
- Take everything you need to coves without facilities, and take your rubbish with you.
- Some beaches have parking fees or access rules that change by season, so look for local notices.

## South-east coves in detail

Caló des Moro lies a short drive from the town of Santanyí, down a signed path from the Es Llombards road. The cove is only a few metres wide, sits between cliffs and holds a few hundred people at most, so it can feel crowded by mid-morning. Cala s'Almunia, a ten-minute walk away, is a larger inlet with a bit more room and a very similar look. Cala Santanyí has a small beach below a natural rock arch, called Es Pontàs, which stands in the sea at the western end of the bay.

Cala Mondragó is the focus of a natural park near Santanyí. A short path from the car park leads to Cala Mondragó and S'Amarador, two bays divided by a rocky headland. The park also has pine woods and a small wetland where birds gather in winter. Cala d'Or, to the north, is a resort of whitewashed villas built around a series of small coves. Further south, Es Trenc and Ses Covetes stand beside the Salobrar de Campos, where traditional salt works are still active.

## North and north-east beaches

The Bay of Alcúdia is the largest on the island. Platja de Muro and Playa de Alcúdia are on the same long curve of sand, with gentle shallow water that can stretch out several metres from the shore. Behind them lies S'Albufera Natural Park, the island's largest wetland, which is good for birdwatching from spring to autumn. Port de Pollença has a calmer town beach by the pine-fringed promenade, and the road to the Cap de Formentor passes Cala Figuera and Platja de Formentor.

Further east, Cala Agulla and Cala Mesquida are broad beaches of fine sand backed by dunes, close to Capdepera and Cala Ratjada. Cala Torta, a wild beach near Artà with no facilities, requires a short walk and rewards you with space. Cala Millor on the east coast is a long, family-friendly resort beach with a promenade and plenty of cafés.

## West and south-west

The south-west of the island, around Andratx, is known for the small harbour of Port d'Andratx and the nearby coves of Cala Llamp and Cala Conills. Sant Elm, at the western tip, offers a small beach looking over to the island of Sa Dragonera, a nature reserve reached by a short boat trip from Sant Elm in season. Illetes and Portals, near Palma, have a mix of sandy beaches and restaurants and attract many residents on weekends.

## Palma's own beaches

Palma is a city with several beaches within reach. Platja de Palma, east of the city, forms a long strip of sand over four kilometres, divided into numbered stops called balnearios. Ciutat Jardí and Can Pastilla are the quieter parts, with family hotels and a promenade. Cala Major, on the west side, is a small sandy cove close to the Marivent Palace. Playa de Palma's balnearios are reached by a bike path that follows the coast, and the coast bus is the easiest way to travel between them.

## When each beach is best

- **Early morning**: Caló des Moro, Cala Varques and Es Trenc, before the day trippers arrive.
- **Midday**: Platja de Muro, Alcúdia and Cala Millor, where there is shade and shelter.
- **Late afternoon**: Cala Agulla, Cala Mesquida and Formentor, when the light is soft.
- **Out of season**: Es Trenc and Cala Mondragó are pleasant for walks from October to May.

## Rules and respect

Mallorca's natural parks, including Es Trenc and Mondragó, have rules to protect dunes and seagrass. Walking on the dunes is forbidden, and camping and campfires are banned. Take litter home and keep dogs on a lead where they are allowed. Lifeguards operate on many beaches in summer, and flags show the water conditions. Many beaches also have wheelchair access and amphibious chairs, which you can request from the lifeguard station.

## Official information

The Balearic government publishes beach water quality results, and town halls post lifeguard schedules on their websites. For national weather warnings, check AEMET at aemet.es, and for emergencies call 112.
