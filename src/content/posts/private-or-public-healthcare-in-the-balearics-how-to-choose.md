Anyone who moves to the Balearic Islands soon hears the same advice from neighbours: get private insurance. Others say the public service is excellent and that insurance is a waste. Both views contain some truth, and the right choice depends on your circumstances. This guide sets out the official facts about public access, explains how private care fits in and lists the questions to ask before you decide. It does not rank hospitals or give prices, since there are no official comparisons for those. It was last checked on 29 October 2026, and it is general information, not advice.

## What the public system gives you

IB-Salut, the Balearic public health service, provides care to residents who are recognised as insured persons or beneficiaries by the national Social Security institute. After registering for an individual health card, you are assigned a family doctor and nurse at your local health centre, with referral to hospital specialists when needed. Emergency care is available through the 112 number and hospital emergency departments. Care is largely free at the point of use, with patients paying a share of the cost of prescription medicines.

The public hospitals named by the Govern and IB-Salut include Son Espases in Palma, Mateu Orfila in Maó and Can Misses in Ibiza. Specialist and complex care for Menorca, Ibiza and Formentera may require travel. IB-Salut runs a scheme that compensates patients for some travel costs when they must go to another island or to the mainland for treatment.

## Who can use it

Workers who pay into Social Security and their families, pensioners and others who qualify under national rules are covered. Residents who are not covered can sign the special agreement, which gives access to the basic common portfolio for a fee of 60 euros a month for those under 65 and 157 euros a month for those aged 65 and over, according to IB-Salut. The agreement requires a continuous year of residence immediately before applying, and registration in a Balearic municipality. It excludes medicines and some other products.

Visitors with a GHIC or EHIC can use the public system for necessary care during a temporary stay. The article on seeing a doctor explains the details.

## What private care offers

Private care is provided by clinics, hospitals and individual practitioners, paid directly or through insurance. People who choose it often cite several reasons:

- Faster access to some appointments and tests.
- The choice of doctor and, in some places, English-speaking staff.
- Different facilities, such as single rooms.

Private insurance varies widely in what it covers, the waiting periods, exclusions for existing conditions and limits on payments. Premiums depend on age and the plan. The official sources do not publish premiums or compare insurers. Spain's insurance regulator and consumer bodies provide general guidance on reading policies.

## Public and private together

Many residents use both. They rely on the public system for primary care, emergencies and complex hospital treatment, and pay for private appointments for specific needs. Some insurance plans only cover outpatient care, and others include hospital stays. If you have private insurance, tell your insurer before treatment so that the claim is accepted. In emergencies, the nearest hospital may be public, and you can be treated there whatever your insurance.

## Who might lean to the public system

- Workers and their families who are already covered through Social Security.
- People with long-term conditions needing continuing care, since the public system offers coordinated follow-up.
- Anyone who prefers a single system for primary care and hospital treatment.

## Who might consider private cover

- Residents who are not covered by Social Security and do not wish to use the special agreement.
- Visitors on long stays who are not eligible for public care.
- People who want a specific provider or faster appointments for particular services.
- Those who need coverage during the first year, before they meet the residence requirement for the agreement.

## Questions to ask

- Am I eligible for the public system? Check with IB-Salut or the Social Security office.
- What does my insurance cover, and what does it exclude?
- Does it cover pre-existing conditions?
- Is there a waiting period before I can claim?
- Does it cover repatriation?
- Where are the nearest hospitals on the island where I live?
- Does my plan allow care on other islands or on the mainland?

## Documents to keep

Keep copies of your health card, insurance policy, contact numbers, medical reports and a list of medicines. Take them to appointments. If you move between countries, ask for a summary of your medical history in English or Spanish.

## Prescriptions

Prescriptions are electronic in the public system. Private doctors issue prescriptions too, and pharmacies accept them. Patients in the public system pay a share of the price, while private patients pay the full price unless insured.

## Looking ahead

Health policy is decided by the Govern and the Spanish government, and both face elections: the general election on 29 November 2026 and the regional elections on 23 May 2027. Rules on access and fees can change, so check IB-Salut's website before making a long-term decision.

## Questions people ask

**Can I use the public system if I also have private insurance?** If you are covered by Social Security, you remain entitled to public care. Having private insurance does not remove that right.

**What happens if I need a hospital on another island?** Public patients can be transferred, and IB-Salut has a scheme to compensate some travel costs. Private insurers set their own rules on transfers.

**Do I need a Spanish bank account to pay for the special agreement?** The pages consulted do not say. Ask IB-Salut about payment methods when you apply.

**How early should I decide?** Before you travel or move, because some insurance has waiting periods, and the special agreement requires a year of residence before you can apply.
