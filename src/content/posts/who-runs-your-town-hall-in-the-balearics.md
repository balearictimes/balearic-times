For most people, the part of government that matters most day to day is the town hall. It issues building and business licences, collects local taxes, runs the local police, looks after the streets and beaches, and keeps the register of residents. The Balearic Islands have 67 municipalities: 53 in Mallorca, eight in Menorca, five in Ibiza and one in Formentera. This guide explains how they work. It does not list every mayor, because the names change with elections and coalition deals, and the official lists are published on each town hall's own website. The rules were last checked on 29 October 2026.

## How a mayor is chosen

Voters do not choose the mayor directly. They vote for a party list in the municipal election, which is held on the fourth Sunday of May every four years under the national electoral law. The last one was on 28 May 2023 and the next is due on 23 May 2027. The number of councillors in each council depends on the population of the municipality, and seats are allocated to lists by the D'Hondt method.

After the election, the new councillors meet and choose the mayor, called the alcalde or alcaldessa in Spanish and batle or batlessa in Catalan. A candidate who wins an absolute majority of councillors becomes mayor. If nobody does, the law gives the post to the head of the list that received the most votes. That rule is why a party that won the most votes can still lose control of a town hall, since a group of other parties can combine to form a majority at the first vote, and why coalition agreements are common after local elections.

## What town halls do

Under the national local government law and the regional rules, a town hall is responsible for:

- **Planning and building**: urban plans, building licences and enforcement against illegal building.
- **Business licences**: opening a bar, restaurant or shop usually involves the town hall, as a separate article in this series explains.
- **Local police**: the Policía Local enforces local ordinances, such as rules on noise, street drinking and beach behaviour.
- **Streets, waste and water**: cleaning, rubbish collection, street lighting and often water supply.
- **Local taxes**: the property tax (IBI), the vehicle tax and the tax on construction.
- **The padrón**: the municipal register of residents. It is the basis for many services, including access to public healthcare as a resident, school places and voting.
- **Social services and culture**: basic social help, libraries, fiestas and local sport.

In the Balearics, the town halls work alongside the island councils and the Govern. Some of the most disputed topics, such as tourist rentals, involve all three. A town hall can, for example, regulate which types of home can be used for holiday lets under its planning rules, while the Govern sets the registration regime and the island council sets the ceiling on tourist places.

## The biggest town halls

Palma is the capital of the community and the largest municipality. The Ajuntament de Palma runs the city, including the city bus company EMT, the old town, the seafront and the beaches such as Ciutat Jardí and Can Pere Antoni. Calvià, on the south-west of Mallorca, covers the resort areas of Magaluf, Santa Ponça and Palmanova. Llucmajor covers part of Playa de Palma and the south coast. Manacor and Inca are the largest inland towns, and Alcúdia, Pollença and Sóller are major tourist centres.

On Ibiza, Eivissa (Ibiza Town), Sant Antoni de Portmany, Santa Eulària des Riu, Sant Josep de sa Talaia and Sant Joan de Labritja are the five municipalities. On Menorca, Maó (Mahón) and Ciutadella are the largest of eight. Formentera has one town hall, which doubles as the island council.

## How to find your mayor and councillors

Each town hall publishes on its website the name of the mayor, the list of councillors and their areas of responsibility, the dates of full council meetings and the minutes. Spanish transparency law requires this. The councils also publish their budgets and the salaries of elected officials. You can also search the Ministry of Territorial Policy's register of local entities and the Govern's directory of municipalities.

If you are a resident and want to take part, you can attend open plenary sessions, file a written request with the town hall's registry, and make complaints through the online sede electrónica, the electronic office. Neighbourhood associations are common, and many councils consult residents on local plans.

## Who can vote in municipal elections

Spanish nationals can vote in municipal elections. So can citizens of other European Union countries who are registered in the electoral roll. Citizens of the United Kingdom can also vote under a reciprocity agreement between Spain and the UK, which keeps the right to vote in municipal elections for British nationals who meet the conditions. A separate article explains how to register and who is eligible. Foreign residents cannot vote in regional or general elections unless they take Spanish nationality.

## Why local politics matters in the Balearics

Many of the disputes in the islands are decided locally. How many tourist beds a town allows, whether a hotel can be extended, how a seafront is organised, how much a town charges for parking, and how it deals with party tourism all fall within local powers. A resident who feels strongly about overcrowding, housing or noise will often find the answer in the town hall's plan or its ordinances before a regional law.

## Practical tips

- Check that you are on the padrón of your municipality. It is free and opens the door to many services.
- Read the local ordinances on noise and public order if you run a business or rent a property. They are published in the BOIB.
- Use the town hall's electronic office for licences and permits.
- If you are applying for a licence, ask for a pre-application meeting with the technical department.
