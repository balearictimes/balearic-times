People in the Balearic Islands vote in several different elections, and the rules are not the same for each one. A resident can be asked to vote for the Parlament in Palma, for an island council, for a town hall, for the Congreso and Senado in Madrid and for the European Parliament. This guide explains the main rules, using the Statute of Autonomy, the regional electoral law and the national electoral law. It was last checked on 29 October 2026.

## The Parlament de les Illes Balears

The regional parliament has 59 deputies. The regional electoral law, Ley 8/1986, sets the number and divides the seats among the four island constituencies as follows:

- Mallorca: 33 deputies
- Menorca: 13 deputies
- Ibiza: 12 deputies
- Formentera: 1 deputy

The Statute of Autonomy says members are elected by proportional representation and serve four years. Seats are allocated within each island using the formula set out in the national electoral law, the D'Hondt method, and only lists that reach 5 per cent of the valid votes cast in the constituency take part in the distribution. The threshold is written into article 12 of the regional law. In Formentera, with a single seat, the contest is in practice between the lists that get most votes on the island.

This system gives the smaller islands more weight than their population alone would. Mallorca has the great majority of the population but a little over half of the seats. That is a deliberate feature of the law, and it is one reason why island identity matters in Balearic politics.

The Parlament approves the budget, passes regional laws and oversees the Govern. After an election, deputies vote to choose the President of the Govern, who then names the consellers. The current President is Margalida Prohens Rigo, according to the Govern's website.

## The island councils

Each island has its own governing institution, the Consell Insular, called Consell de Mallorca, Consell de Menorca, Consell d'Eivissa and Consell de Formentera. The Statute says they hold the government, administration and representation of their island. Their members are chosen on the same day as the Parlament and town hall elections, and voters are normally given a separate ballot for each. A separate article in this series explains who runs each council.

## Town halls

There are 67 municipalities in the Balearics: 53 in Mallorca, 8 in Menorca, 5 in Ibiza and one in Formentera. In a municipal election, voters choose a party list. The number of councillors depends on the population of the municipality, as the national electoral law sets out. Mayors are chosen by the councillors. If a candidate obtains an absolute majority of councillors, he or she becomes mayor. If nobody does, the head of the list that won most votes takes the post.

## Madrid: Congreso and Senado

For the Congreso, the Illes Balears is a single constituency with eight deputies. Voters pick a closed list, and seats are allocated by the D'Hondt method. For the Senado, Mallorca elects three senators and Menorca, Ibiza and Formentera one each. Voters pick candidates, not a list. The Parlament also designates senators who represent the community. A general election is due on 29 November 2026, as described in a separate article.

## The next elections

The dates follow from the law:

- **29 November 2026**: general election (Congreso and Senado).
- **23 May 2027**: regional, island and municipal elections. The electoral law sets these for the fourth Sunday of May every four years, and the previous ones were on 28 May 2023.
- **2029**: the next European Parliament election, following the cycle of 2024.

The date of the May 2027 election is fixed by law. Unlike the general election, the Balearic President cannot call it early unless the Parlament is dissolved by a formal decree, which the Statute allows in some circumstances.

## How you find out where to vote

Spanish voters are registered in the electoral roll (censo electoral), which is built from the municipal register (padrón) and the civil registry. Each election has a period in which you can consult the roll and make a claim. Voters receive a card with the address of their polling station. The Ministry of the Interior and the Instituto Nacional de Estadística publish online tools to check the roll. Polling stations are normally open from 09:00 to 20:00, and you need an identity document with a photograph.

## How results are counted

On election night, the first count is provisional and is run by the Ministry of the Interior. The official count is made by the Provincial Electoral Boards, who review the results from each polling station in the days afterwards. The Junta Electoral Provincial, an independent panel that includes judges, supervises the process, and appeals can be taken to the courts.

## What you can read in the original texts

The consolidated Statute of Autonomy on the BOE website is the clearest starting point for the structure of the Parlament and Govern. The regional electoral law is short and readable. The national electoral law, the LOREG, is long but contains the formulas and the rules for voting.

## Questions people ask

**Why do voters get several ballots?** Because the Parlament, the island council and the town hall are separate elections held on the same day in May.

**Can I vote if I am temporarily away?** Spanish voters can apply for a postal vote, using the procedure published by Correos and the electoral authorities for each election.

**What is a closed and blocked list?** Voters choose a whole party list in the order the party set, without changing it. It is used for the Congreso and the Parlament.

**Who counts the votes?** Polling station members chosen by lot count at the end of the day, and the Electoral Boards check the results in the days after.
