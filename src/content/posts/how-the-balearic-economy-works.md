The economy of the Balearic Islands is easy to describe in one word, tourism, but the full picture has more parts. This guide explains how the islands earn their living, what the official figures say and why the calendar shapes so much of the work. The main source is the annual report of the Consell Econòmic i Social (CES) of the Balearic Islands for 2024, supported by data from the Instituto Nacional de Estadística, the regional statistics office IBESTAT and the Balearic tourism agency. Figures were last checked on 29 October 2026.

## A service economy

According to the CES, services make up 85.8 per cent of economic activity in the Balearics. In terms of employment, services provide 81.6 per cent of jobs, construction 10.5 per cent, industry 7.2 per cent and the primary sector, mainly farming and fishing, 0.7 per cent. The industrial base is small, and agriculture is a small share of output, although it has a role in the landscape and local food.

Within services, tourism leads. The Balearic tourism agency reports that tourism accounted for 19.1 per cent of all workers registered with Social Security in the fourth quarter of 2025, with 94,706 affiliates directly in tourism activities and 8,825 registered businesses. That is the narrow definition. A wider view includes construction work on hotels, shops that sell to visitors, transport, retail and food supply, which all depend on the same demand.

## Growth

The CES reports that the gross value added of the islands grew 4 per cent in 2024. Nominal GDP per person rose 10.9 per cent to 34,381 euros, which the report describes as the largest increase in Spain that year. The tourist numbers behind this are set out in a separate article: 18.7 million tourists in 2024, up 4.9 per cent on 2023 and 13.7 per cent above 2019.

## Work

Employment in the Balearics set records in 2024. Average annual affiliation to Social Security was 607,250, up 3.0 per cent. The average number of unemployed people was 33,980, down 6.7 per cent. The unemployment rate averaged 8.2 per cent, the lowest since 2007, according to the CES.

The latest Labour Force Survey from the INE, for the third quarter of 2026, gives the Balearics an unemployment rate of 4.63 per cent, the lowest of any region in Spain that quarter. Spain as a whole stood at 10.18 per cent, up 0.31 points on the previous quarter. The third quarter is the summer peak in the islands, so the figure reflects the high season.

## The seasonal pattern

The CES report shows how strongly the year is divided. In July 2024, the number of workers affiliated to Social Security was 34.9 per cent higher than in December. The summer workforce has to be hired, housed and trained within a few weeks, and then much of it leaves in the autumn. That produces unusual patterns in rents, in the number of temporary contracts and in demand for public services at different times of year.

Seasonal work in Spain is often organised through a type of permanent contract with discontinuous activity, the fijo discontinuo, which lets a worker be called back each season. A separate article explains the seasonal job market.

## Labour costs

The average labour cost per worker in the islands was 2,254.9 euros per month in 2024, according to the CES. That was 2.0 per cent below the national average, although it rose 4.8 per cent compared with 2023. Labour costs include wages plus the employer's social security contributions, so they do not tell us the net pay a worker takes home. The national minimum wage in 2026 is set by Real Decreto 126/2026 at 1,221 euros per month in 14 payments, which is 17,094 euros per year.

## Where the money comes from

The tourism spending figures show how much comes from outside. Visitors spent 22,381 million euros in 2024, up 12.2 per cent, or 187 euros per day on average, according to the CES. That money pays for hotel staff, restaurants, taxis, shops and the suppliers behind them. It also pays taxes, including VAT and the sustainable tourism tax, which the Balearic government collects.

## Vulnerabilities

The CES and other bodies point to several weak points, which are widely discussed in official documents:

- **Concentration.** The economy depends heavily on one sector. A fall in visitor numbers, a spike in fuel prices or an air traffic disruption can hit many businesses at once.
- **Housing.** The cost of housing makes it harder to recruit staff. This is explained in another article.
- **Environmental limits.** Water, energy, land and waste are limited on small islands, and the regional government has been working on climate and energy plans.
- **Seasonality.** Income arrives in a few months, and many workers have gaps in the year.

## Where the economy is trying to diversify

Regional policy has encouraged technology firms, renewable energy, film production, yachting and nautical services, and remote working. A separate article on technology covers the start-up scene. The Govern's department of Business, Self-Employed and Energy and its department of Economy, Finance and Innovation are responsible for these policies.

## Questions readers ask

**Is tourism really the biggest part of the economy?** The official figures put services at 85.8 per cent of economic activity, and tourism directly accounts for 19.1 per cent of registered workers. Many other jobs depend on it indirectly.

**Why is unemployment so low in summer?** The third-quarter labour survey captures the peak of the season, when hotels and restaurants are fully staffed. The annual average is higher, as the 2024 figure of 8.2 per cent shows.

**Who sets economic policy?** Both the national government and the Balearic Govern. The Govern's departments for Economy, Finance and Innovation, and for Business, Self-Employed and Energy, handle regional policy, while taxes and many labour rules are national.

**Where do I find the latest numbers?** The article on the economy in numbers lists the main sources and the latest releases.
