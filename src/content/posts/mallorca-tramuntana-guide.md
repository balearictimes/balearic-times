The Serra de Tramuntana runs along the north-west of Mallorca for about 90 kilometres. Its limestone ridges, terraced hillsides and old stone paths were listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage cultural landscape in 2011, and the area is the main reason many people visit the island outside the beach season.

## Valldemossa

Valldemossa is a hillside village of stone houses and flower-filled streets. Its Carthusian monastery is where the composer Frédéric Chopin and the writer George Sand spent the winter of 1838 to 1839. The village is popular with day trippers, so go early in the morning or later in the afternoon.

## Deià

Deià sits on a hill between the mountains and the sea. The writer Robert Graves lived there for decades, and many artists and musicians have followed him. A footpath leads down to a small pebble cove with a couple of simple restaurants.

## Sóller and Port de Sóller

Sóller is a market town in a valley famous for its orange and lemon groves. A vintage tram connects it with Port de Sóller, a calm harbour with a beach and seafront cafés. The historic train from Palma is part of the appeal.

## Sa Calobra and Torrent de Pareis

The road to Sa Calobra is one of the island's most dramatic drives, with tight bends that loop down to the coast. At the bottom, a short walk through tunnels leads to the mouth of a gorge, where the Torrent de Pareis meets the sea. In summer the road is crowded with coaches and cars, and many people take the boat from Port de Sóller instead.

## Lluc and the high ground

Lluc is a monastery and pilgrimage site in the heart of the range, with a small museum and walking routes. The highest peak, Puig Major, rises to roughly 1,445 metres, though the summit is occupied by a military installation and is not open to the public.

## Walking the GR 221

The GR 221, also called the Dry Stone Route, is a long-distance footpath that runs through the Tramuntana from Port d'Andratx in the south-west to Pollença in the north-east. It is divided into stages with refuges for overnight stays. Many walkers do individual sections as day hikes. Spring and autumn are the most comfortable seasons for hiking.

## Practical advice

- Summer heat can make mountain walks dangerous. Start early, carry plenty of water and take a hat.
- Weather can change quickly in the mountains, and paths become slippery after rain. Check the forecast.
- Many mountain roads are narrow and busy with cyclists. Drive slowly and give them space.
- Parking in popular villages is limited. Public buses serve some of them, which can be easier than driving.
- Respect private land. Many paths cross farms and have gates that should be left as you found them.

## Village by village

Andratx and Port d'Andratx mark the south-western end of the range. Port d'Andratx has a sheltered harbour lined with restaurants, and the village of Sant Elm, nearby, looks over to the island of Sa Dragonera. Banyalbufar, a village of terraced slopes above the sea, was built by the Moors for tomatoes and wine grapes, and its terraces called marjades are still visible. Estellencs, further north, is a small village with a rocky cove below it, reached by a steep path.

Valldemossa sits at 400 metres in a bowl of hills. The Real Cartuja is the main sight, and the cells where Chopin and George Sand stayed are open to visitors. The village also has a pharmacy museum and the birthplace of Santa Catalina Thomàs, the island's only saint. Deià, on the coast, is a short drive away, and it holds the house of the poet Robert Graves, which now serves as a museum.

Sóller, a market town, is the starting point for the old train and the tram. The Jardí Botànic de Sóller, a botanical garden, is a pleasant stop. Fornalutx, nearby, is a village of stone houses and is often named among the most beautiful in Spain. Biniaraix is a hamlet with a steep stone path, the Barranc de Biniaraix, that climbs through olive terraces and is a popular walk.

## Lluc, Pollença and the north

Lluc is the island's main religious centre. The monastery houses a statue of the Virgin known as La Moreneta, and the choir of boy singers, Els Blavets, performs in the church. The area is a hub for hiking routes and has a campsite and a hostel for walkers. The mountains of the north rise above Pollença, where the 365 steps of the Calvari climb to a small chapel with views over the town.

The Cap de Formentor, at the end of the road from Port de Pollença, is a narrow peninsula with a lighthouse at the end. The Mirador des Colomer is a viewpoint on the way up, where cliffs plunge 200 metres into the sea. In summer, the road is closed to private cars in the middle of the day, and a bus runs instead.

## Walks to try

- **Barranc de Biniaraix**: a short, stone-paved path from Biniaraix up towards the Cúber reservoir. It is shaded in part and suits families.
- **Sa Costera, near Deià**: a coastal path from Deià to Port de Sóller, with views of the sea. It takes a few hours and needs good shoes.
- **Torrent de Pareis**: a gorge walk from Sa Calobra to Escorca. It is a demanding route and should only be tackled with the right gear and in dry weather.
- **Castell d'Alaró**: a hill-top castle reached by a path from the village of Alaró, with a small restaurant at the top.
- **Puig de Maria, Pollença**: a short hill walk to a small monastery with a view over the bay.

## The GR 221 stages

The GR 221, or Ruta de Pedra en Sec, runs for roughly 140 kilometres in around eight stages. The refuges at Tossals Verds, Son Amer and Pont Romà, run by the Consell de Mallorca, offer simple lodging for walkers and must be booked in advance. The best seasons are March to May and October to November. In July and August, the heat makes long days on the trail dangerous.

## Wildlife and nature

The Tramuntana is home to the black vulture, the Mallorcan midwife toad and wild goats. Pine and holm oak forest covers much of the higher ground, and the hillsides are lined with centuries-old olive trees. The coast is rocky with few beaches, and swimming is best from small coves such as Cala Deià and Cala Tuent.

## Heritage and traditions

The UNESCO listing recognises the way farmers and monks shaped the mountains with terraces, irrigation channels and stone walls. Many of the old water systems of Moorish origin are still in use. Dry-stone building, a skill passed down through generations, is celebrated by local councils, and workshops teach the craft.

## Planning your visit

- Allow a full day for the drive from Palma to Valldemossa, Deià and Sóller.
- Fill the tank before you leave, since petrol stations are few.
- Mobile signal can be patchy in the valleys.
- Wear sturdy shoes, even on short walks.
- Respect local residents and private property.

## Official information

The Consell de Mallorca publishes information on the GR 221, refuge bookings and hiking safety. The Spanish weather agency AEMET, at aemet.es, issues warnings for the mountains, and the emergency number is 112.
