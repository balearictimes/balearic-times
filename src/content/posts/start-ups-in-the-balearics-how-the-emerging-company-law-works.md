The Balearic Islands are better known for hotels than for software, but a technology and start-up scene exists, centred on Palma and supported by public bodies and technology parks. Founders there use the same legal framework as the rest of Spain, and the key piece is the national law on emerging companies, Ley 28/2022. This article explains what the law says, based on the text published in the Boletín Oficial del Estado, and points to where islands-based founders can look for help. It is general information, not legal or tax advice. It was last checked on 29 October 2026, and amendments to the law may have been made since it was passed.

## What counts as an emerging company

Article 3 of the law lists conditions that a company must meet all at once. It must:

- Be no more than five years old, counted from the registration of its incorporation deed. The limit is seven years for companies in biotechnology, energy, industry and other strategic sectors, or those developing their own technology designed entirely in Spain.
- Not result from a merger, split or conversion of companies that are not themselves emerging.
- Not distribute dividends and not be listed on a regulated market.
- Have its registered office, domicile or permanent establishment in Spain.
- Have at least 60 per cent of its workforce employed under Spanish contracts.
- Develop an innovative venture with a scalable business model. Article 3.2 says the innovation must solve a problem or substantially improve a situation, and carry a risk of technological, industrial or business model failure.

Article 6 says the benefits end if annual turnover exceeds 10 million euros, if the company stops meeting the conditions, when it reaches five or seven years, if it is dissolved or acquired by a non-emerging company, or if it causes significant environmental damage. Founders or directors with unpaid tax or Social Security debts, or with certain criminal convictions, are excluded.

## How certification works

ENISA, the national innovation company, assesses whether a business is innovative and scalable. Article 4 lists its criteria: degree of innovation, market attractiveness, stage of life, scalability, competition, team, dependence on suppliers and customers, and reputational or ethical risk. It must decide within three months of a complete application, and if it does not, silence counts as approval. Once the company is recorded as emerging in the Registro Mercantil, article 5 says that registration is both necessary and enough to use the law, although the tax authority can later check that the conditions were met.

## The benefits

The text lists several advantages:

- **Corporate tax.** The rate is 15 per cent in the first tax year with positive taxable income and the three following years, instead of the general rate, which the Agencia Tributaria lists as 25 per cent in 2026.
- **Deferral of tax debt.** The company can defer, without guarantees, the payment of the tax debt for the first two profitable years, for 12 and 6 months.
- **No instalment payments** on account of corporate tax while the company remains emerging.
- **Employee share options.** The exemption for shares given to staff rises from 12,000 to 50,000 euros a year.
- **Investor relief.** The personal income tax deduction for investing in new companies rises from 30 to 50 per cent, with a base limit of 100,000 euros a year.
- **Company law.** There is no obligation to dissolve for losses during the first three years, and incorporation can be done in one step through the electronic single document, with reduced fees.
- **Testing.** Regulated sectors can offer test licences for up to a year.

The law also created the visa and residence authorisation for international teleworkers, covered in a separate article on digital nomads. The preamble describes a visa of up to one year, and a residence authorisation of up to three years, renewable for two, with permanent residence possible after five.

## Setting up in the islands

An emerging company is first an ordinary company. A founder who wants to base it in the Balearics follows the steps for forming an SL, which the article on forming a company sets out, and then applies for emerging status. The ordinary requirements on licences, Social Security and tax registration apply.

## Where to find support

- **Technology park.** ParcBIT, in Palma, is the technology park that hosts companies and public bodies. The Govern has installed a solar canopy there, which shows its use as a showcase for public projects.
- **Regional government.** The department of Economy, Finance and Innovation, led by the First Vice President, Antoni Costa Costa, covers innovation policy, and the department of Business, Self-Employed and Energy handles support for small business, according to the Govern's website. Calls for grants are published on caib.es and in the BOIB.
- **Chambers of commerce.** They run advice services and events, as the article on business support explains.
- **ENISA and ICEX.** National bodies that provide certification, financing information and export help.
- **PAE points.** Advice points that help with the electronic incorporation process.

## What the sources do not say

The official texts used here describe the framework, not the size of the local scene. They do not give the number of start-ups in the Balearics, the amount of investment they have raised or the names of the leading companies. Readers who want those figures should look for official counts from ENISA or the Govern, or from the chambers of commerce, and treat private league tables with caution.

## Practical advice for founders

- Decide early whether emerging company status fits your plans. The five-year window and the ban on dividends are real constraints.
- Speak to an adviser about the tax effects of employee share options.
- Keep records showing that you meet the 60 per cent Spanish workforce requirement.
- Plan for housing costs when hiring, since the article on housing explains the pressures on staff.
