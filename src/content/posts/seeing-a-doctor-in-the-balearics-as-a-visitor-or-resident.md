Getting sick on holiday, or in your first months of living in the islands, is stressful enough without having to work out how the system works. The Balearic Islands offer both public and private care. Which route you take, and what you pay, depends on your status. This guide sets out the options using IB-Salut's pages and the guidance that governments publish on health cover abroad. It is general information, not medical advice, and rules for cross-border cover can change, so check the official pages for your own situation. It was last checked on 29 October 2026.

## If it is an emergency

Call 112, the European emergency number, from any phone. Operators in the islands can handle calls in English. You can also go directly to the emergency department of a public hospital (urgencias), where staff treat urgent cases first. The main public hospitals are Son Espases in Palma, Mateu Orfila in Maó and Can Misses in Ibiza Town. Formentera has a health centre in Sant Francesc and transfers serious cases to Ibiza.

## If you are a visitor from the EU or the UK

Visitors from the European Union carry a European Health Insurance Card (EHIC), and visitors from the United Kingdom carry a Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC). These cards give access to state-provided healthcare that becomes medically necessary during a temporary stay, on the same terms as residents of the country you visit. That means you may still pay what residents pay for some items, such as part of the price of medicines. The cards do not cover private treatment, repatriation or the cost of getting home, so travel insurance is still advisable. The UK government publishes how to apply for a GHIC, and the cards are free through the official site only.

To use the card, go to a public health centre or hospital and show it with your passport. For minor problems, a pharmacy is often quicker. Pharmacists can advise on sunburn, stings, stomach upsets and many small complaints.

## If you are a visitor from elsewhere

Visitors from countries with no health agreement with Spain should have comprehensive travel insurance. Public hospitals treat emergencies, but they may charge foreign patients who are not covered. Keep receipts, and ask for a report from the doctor to support an insurance claim.

## If you have just moved

Residents who work and pay Social Security contributions, and their families, are normally covered by the public system. The IB-Salut access page explains that you need to be recognised as insured or a beneficiary by the national Social Security institute, and that you then request an individual health card (TSI). You will usually need to be registered in your town hall's register of residents, the padrón.

Retired people from the UK who live in Spain may be covered through the UK's own arrangements. The UK government publishes the rules for people who receive a UK state pension, and the Spanish Social Security has to register the certificate. Check the UK government's pages before relying on any particular route.

## If you are resident but not covered

IB-Salut offers the special agreement, the convenio especial. It gives access to the basic common portfolio of services for a monthly fee, listed by IB-Salut as 60 euros a month for those under 65 and 157 euros for those aged 65 or over. You must live in the islands, be registered in a Balearic municipality, prove at least one continuous year of residence in Spain, the EU, the EEA, Switzerland or the UK, and apply to IB-Salut. The agreement excludes medicines, orthoprosthetic products and non-urgent medical transport. Decisions are due within a month.

IB-Salut has also restored access to care for foreigners in an irregular situation who lack resources, according to its own news pages, and a Govern notice is headed as authorising an agreement between the health department and a body called UBES on health cover for temporary EU and British citizens. This article could not read the detail of that agreement, so it does not describe it. The IB-Salut and Govern websites explain both measures, and a person who thinks they may qualify should ask at a health centre.

## What you pay

Public care is mostly free at the point of use for those covered. Patients pay part of the cost of prescription medicines, based on their income and status. Private care is paid in full or through insurance. Fees vary by clinic and are not set by the public authorities. This article does not quote prices, because no official fee table exists for private care.

## Seeing a doctor step by step

- **Minor problem while visiting:** ask your hotel or host for the nearest health centre, or visit a pharmacy.
- **Needing a doctor:** go to the nearest health centre or private clinic. Take your passport, your insurance details and your GHIC or EHIC.
- **Emergency:** call 112 or go to urgencias.
- **After treatment:** keep all reports, receipts and prescriptions.
- **Claiming from insurance:** contact your insurer as soon as possible, because many require notification.

## Language

Staff speak Catalan and Spanish, and many speak English in tourist areas. If you do not speak Spanish, bring a friend, write down your symptoms and medicines in advance, and ask for a written report.

## Prescriptions and medicines

Take enough of your regular medicines for the trip, in their original packaging, with a doctor's letter if they are controlled drugs. Pharmacies in Spain dispense many medicines without a prescription, but some require one. Staff can check whether your medicine is available locally.

## Questions people ask

**Do I pay at the hospital with a GHIC or EHIC?** You are treated on the same terms as residents. Residents pay little or nothing for most treatment, but they do pay part of the cost of prescription medicines.

**Can I go to any health centre?** Visitors can use the nearest public health centre or hospital for necessary care. Bring your passport and card.

**What if I lose my card?** Ask your health authority at home for a replacement or a provisional certificate, and keep your travel insurance details to hand.
